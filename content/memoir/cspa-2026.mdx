In May 2026 I presented a paper at an international conference for the first time. It was the 22nd IEEE International Colloquium on Signal Processing & Its Applications, CSPA 2026, held on the 1st and 2nd of May. I didn't need a plane ticket for it. I presented virtually, from home.

The paper was *Open-Source LLMs for Evidence-Grounded Clinical Question Answering: A RAG Framework Based on the 2024 ESC Atrial Fibrillation Guidelines*. The question behind it is simple to say and hard to get right: can a language model answer a clinician's question using only what the guidelines actually say, and can you show that it did? We built a retrieval-augmented pipeline over the ESC atrial-fibrillation guidelines and compared three open-source models inside it, LLaMA 3, Phi-3 Mini and Qwen3, on 20 clinical queries.

## Presenting alone

My co-authors didn't join the session, so it was just me, my screen and the presentation. I was nervous. When you present virtually you don't get a room to read. You talk, you watch the clock, and you trust that the people on the other end are following along.

I did it anyway, and that matters more to me than how polished it was. Months of work, from building the retrieval pipeline to choosing evaluation metrics and catching the one answer where a model invented something clinically significant, came down to explaining it clearly to strangers. Saying it out loud to an audience that knows the field made the work feel finished in a way the camera-ready PDF didn't.
